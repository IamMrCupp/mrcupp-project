# Taking work

Short version: I do board-level repair, and I'm taking work.
[Here's the page](/page/electronics-repair/).

Longer version below, because I think the *why* matters more than the pitch.

## Most repair isn't repair

Take a laptop somewhere with a dead port and you'll usually get a quote for a logic board.
Not a component — the board. The diagnosis stops at "which module do we swap," because that's
the model the industry is built around: replacement at the largest convenient unit, billed
accordingly.

Sometimes that's genuinely right. Often it's a $12 part and forty minutes with a microscope,
and the board goes in a bin instead.

**I'd rather find the one part that actually failed and fix that.** That's board-level repair.
It's slower per job and it doesn't scale, which is exactly why the shops don't do it — and
also why it's the part I find worth doing.

## How this happened

I've spent twenty-odd years keeping systems alive at the software and network layer. At some
point the same instinct went physical: instead of a stack trace, a fault on a board; instead
of a bad deploy, a rail that's pulling current it shouldn't be. Same job — figure out what's
actually wrong instead of restarting the thing and hoping.

The vintage machines pulled hardest. Recapping a board from the 80s and watching it boot
again is a very specific kind of satisfying, and it's the direct line back to why I got into
computers at all.

Then the bench filled up. Microscope, hot air, thermal camera, a short-finder, a wall of
instruments — and enough of it that
[I started building tooling for it](/page/benchhud/) and
[printing organizers for it](/post/2026-08-03-3d-printer-models/). At some point a hobby with
this much equipment behind it should probably do some work for other people.

## What I take

Microsoldering — fine-pitch and BGA rework, lifted pads, broken traces, connector
replacement. Component-level diagnosis on logic boards. Regular computer repair too, the
diagnosis-and-fix work that doesn't need a scope. Vintage restoration and recapping. ECU work
is on the roadmap, not offered yet.

And a few things I won't take, which I've put on the page as plainly as I can: boards someone
else has already had a go at, anything still under manufacturer warranty, and jobs where the
data is the real objective — that's a recovery lab, and going in with a soldering iron first
can make their job harder.

Saying no clearly up front is part of the offer. It's how you avoid wasting someone's money
finding out.

## How it works

No ticketing system, no storefront. Tell me what you've got and I'll be straight with you
about whether I can fix it, what it'll take, and when the answer is no. **No-fix, no-fee on
diagnosis.** The usual caveat with any board work applies: back up anything you can't afford
to lose. I'll be careful, but I can't be responsible for data on a device that's already
failing.

The [Repair Services page](/page/electronics-repair/) has a button that opens an email with
the useful questions already laid out — device, symptom, what changed, what you've tried.
Fill in what you know, skip what you don't. Photos of the board help more than anything else
you can send.

That's it. The bench is set up, the lights are on, and I'd like to fix your thing.
